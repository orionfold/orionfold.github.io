## What Flow does

**Bring in a document too long to read. Publish your own guide to it the same day.**
Orionfold Flow turns a long web page into a folder you can work in. **File ▸ New from Web Page** reads the page. Past 40,000 words it tells you so. *Too long for one document. Flow will bring it in as 38 documents.* One button brings in every part. Each part keeps the page's address as its source, with its pictures beside it. Ask Chat to make the folder a Domain, and it writes the README. That page says what the folder is, who wrote it, its licence, the order to read it in and the questions it answers. Then Ideas goes section by section and offers a skill wherever a picture would say it faster. Your reading of the document becomes a figure in it, and you keep or discard each one in Review Changes. When the guide is ready, Publish sends the folder to GitHub. Anyone can bring it into Flow from there and ask it questions. The filing stays the source. The guide is yours.

## The answer first

1. **Make a folder for the Domain** in the sidebar. Here it is `anthropic-s1-analyst`, inside a folder for your Domains.
2. **Choose File ▸ New from Web Page**, paste the page's address, and read it. Past 40,000 words, Flow offers **Bring In All 38…**.
3. **Untick *in a new folder*** so the parts go straight into your Domain's folder, then press **Add**. The parts arrive with a README that lists them.
4. **Select the folder and press ⌘K.** Ask Chat to make the folder a Domain. It writes a README with a title, author, version, licence, summary, the questions the Domain answers, a reading order, and the notes a reader should see first. Keep it in **Review Changes**.
5. **Open Ideas (⌘3)** on a part. Press a suggestion, read the skill's sheet, and press **Run Skill**. Keep or discard what it draws in **Review Changes**. Repeat wherever a figure helps.
6. **Switch the README to Canvas** to see what the Domain is made from and what depends on it.
7. **Choose Publish ▸ GitHub Repository**, type `owner/name/folder`, and press **Publish to GitHub**. Others bring it in with **File ▸ New from GitHub…**.

## The job

A public filing is written for regulators and lawyers. A retail investor wants five things from it. How does the company make money? What does it depend on? Who controls it? What does a share buy? What could go wrong? The answers are all in there, spread across a quarter of a million words. Reading it once takes a weekend. Writing down how you read it, so someone else can follow, is a second job most people never get to. And a PDF of notes loses the filing it came from.

A Domain keeps both. It holds the source, in parts you can open, and your reading of it, as documents that link back. Because a Domain is a folder of Markdown files, it publishes as one. The person who brings it in gets the same parts, the same guide and the same links.

## Bring in a page too long for one document

New from Web Page read the S-1 from its sec.gov address and counted the words before writing anything. Past 40,000 words, it offers a folder instead. That is the length where Flow measured typing starting to lag. **Bring In All 38…** lists the parts under the filing's own sections. Each part is named for its heading and carries the page's address in its front matter. The pictures it shows sit in an `assets` folder.

The task's footer adds the parts to the folder you choose. With *in a new folder* ticked, they go into a subfolder named for the page. Unticked, as here, they land in the Domain's own folder. That is what made this folder a Domain rather than a folder inside one.

The parts are a snapshot. Flow brings them in once, and *Keep it updated* has no effect on them yet.

## Chat writes the front page

With the folder selected, Chat works inside it. New files land there, and Chat can read and change any document in the folder. Asked to make the folder a Domain, it rewrote the README the split had made. It added front matter with a title, an author, a version, a licence and a summary. It added five questions a retail investor should ask of this filing. The body says what the Domain is and sets a reading order in seven steps. It also names a conflict a careful reader would raise. An Anthropic model is analysing Anthropic's own filing. The README says so, and says that nothing in it is investment advice. The full list of parts stays at the end.

Every link in the reading order opens a part that exists. Chat was also plain about how it built the order. It worked from the part titles and three parts it read, not from all 38. Check its order against the parts before you rely on it. The README is the one document worth reading closely, because every reader starts there.

## What the guide knows that the filing does not say

A Domain earns its place when it tells a reader something the source does not hand them. By the end of the afternoon the README alone had grown to 4,629 words. Most of those words are the author's reading, not the filing's text.

- **Five questions, each answered across the whole filing.** Take *Is revenue growing faster than the cost of the compute behind it?* The answer draws on the summary financials, the prospectus summary and the business section at once. Each fact links to the part it came from. One answer gathers what the filing spreads over thousands of words.
- **Comparisons the filing leaves to the reader.** Set the filing's own figures side by side. The first half of 2026 is the first period in its table where revenue ($16,286 million) is larger than compute and infrastructure cost ($8,945 million). The README says so in one line, and a reader can check it against the table it cites.
- **A list of what is still blank.** This is the first S-1, and many of its figures stay empty until the offering is priced. That includes the price range, the share counts, dilution, the capitalisation table, the total of compute commitments and the lock-up releases. The README lists ten of these gaps. For each one it gives what the filing says and where to look next, such as a later amended filing or the final prospectus. "The filing is incomplete" becomes a checklist for the next amendment.
- **Figures drawn from the numbers.** Cash on hand against capital raised. Revenue against costs. Growth rates to scale. Who elects the board. Who Anthropic competes with. Each figure comes from the filing's own words and sits next to the passage it reads. The summary financials alone carry seven charts.
- **The reader's warnings, in the open.** Claude helped draft the guide, and the filing is Anthropic's own. The README calls that a plain conflict of interest. It tells the reader to weigh the risk factors and the financial statements over any summary, and says that nothing in it is investment advice.

The filing stays untouched in its parts. Everything above is the author's layer on top of it, and every claim in that layer points back to the part it rests on.

## Publish it as a Flow Domain

![Publish on the README with GitHub Repository chosen. anthropic-s1-analyst, folder, 41 documents, 22 charts, 257,799 words. Title Anthropic S-1 Analyst, subtitle An Analyst's Guide for Retail Investors, and an author. Destination orionfold/flow/flow-domains/anthropic-s1-analyst, with Publishing the folder to GitHub…](../../assets/flow/domains/anthropic-s1-analyst/07-publishing-to-github.webp)

**Publish ▸ GitHub Repository** sends the folder's documents and pictures, as one version, into the repository and folder you name. Every other file in that repository stays as it is. The title, subtitle and author you type are saved to the README. The token needs one permission on that repository, *Contents: Read and write*. GitHub adds *Metadata* itself. The publish landed as one commit of 84 files, and every one matched the file on the author's Mac byte for byte. Flow's own bookkeeping files stayed behind.

A reader gets the whole Domain, with the parts, the guide and the figures. Publishing the full text of a public filing is the author's choice. A guide can just as well link to the source and quote only what it draws.

## What a Domain gives the person who brings it in

This walk ended at the publish. The rest of what a Domain offers already ships in Flow, though none of it was used on this Domain today.

- **They bring it in with one address.** **File ▸ New from GitHub…** takes `github.com/orionfold/flow/tree/main/flow-domains/anthropic-s1-analyst`. It shows how many files the Domain holds, its version and its licence, and opens it on its README. When the author publishes a new version, a blue arrow appears beside the folder in the reader's sidebar. They see what changed, document by document, and nothing changes until they choose.
- **Chat answers from the Domain, not from memory.** Before each reply, Chat searches the folder for the question and answers from the passages it finds. It links each fact to its part and lists those parts under *From this folder*. When the folder does not say, Chat says so. Because this README carries a title and questions, an empty Chat on it reads *Answers from* the Domain's title and offers its questions as buttons.
- **Search finds every mention.** **Search** (⇧⌘F) looks across every part at once. *Similar to…* lists the parts that share rare words with the one open. A risk named in the summary can be found in its full section.
- **Their own agents can read it.** With Flow Pro, Settings ▸ General ▸ Your agents opens a door for the Claude Code or Codex they run in Terminal. The agent can read a Domain's summary and reading order, search its documents, and propose a new one. The proposal arrives in Review Changes under the agent's name, to keep or revert.

A filing read once is gone by next week. A filing turned into a Domain can be asked, searched, handed to an agent and updated, by anyone the author shares it with.
